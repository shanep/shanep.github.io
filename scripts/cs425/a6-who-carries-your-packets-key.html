Say this at the start

"The Internet is not one network, it is more than 70,000 of them, and Boise State is one. Today we find out which one, who it pays to reach everybody else, and what the rest of the world sees when it looks back at us. Everyone ssh onyx." Then hand out the sheet.

Say "The address goes in backwards, like a reverse DNS lookup, because DNS names get more specific to the left."
Prefix. AS 46662, BSU-AS, announces 132.178.0.0/16: 216 = 65,536 addresses. Shorter than the /25, and it covers Onyx's /25 and every other campus subnet.
Q1. Aggregation: one route for the whole campus keeps every BGP table on the Internet smaller, and the outside world does not need to know how campus carves up its block. Only routers inside AS 46662 know the /25, and they learn it from the intra-AS protocol (OSPF or similar, 5.3). BGP carries only the /16 (5.4).

Say "Read a path right to left: that is the order the route was passed along, starting from us."
Neighbors. Two, both providers: AS 6461 Zayo and AS 46435 IRON (Idaho Regional Optical Network). Of 298 paths, 258 come in through Zayo and 40 through IRON.
Q3. Two providers is multihoming: if one link or provider fails, BGP withdraws those routes and traffic shifts to the other. No right neighbors means nobody reaches the Internet through Boise State and it announces only its own /16: it is a stub AS, not a transit AS, and its providers are paid to carry its traffic. A route whose AS-PATH already holds 6461 has been through Zayo once, so Zayo rejects it: that is AS-PATH loop detection (5.4).
Fast group  Why do most of the world's routers pick Zayo? Route selection is local preference first, then path length. IRON is a regional network that buys its own transit (its paths run through Lumen, 3356 46435 46662), so the route through IRON is usually one AS longer than the route through Zayo. That is the likely story, not something the data proves.

Answers. Google, about 11 ms, versus about 52 ms to Salt Lake City. AS path 46662 6461 15169: 3 ASes, so Zayo hands traffic straight to Google.
Q4. 11 ms round trip is at most about 1,100 km each way, and less in practice. Because 8.8.8.8 is anycast, you reach whichever copy BGP gives you the best route to: Zayo hears 8.8.8.8 from Google in many places and picks one by its own policy. On the XMission path, 27 ms at the first Lumen router means the packet went a long way before Zayo handed it to Lumen, probably to some city where the two connect, then back to Salt Lake. BGP picks paths by policy and AS hops, not by kilometers. The mtr lines with more than one address at hops 10 and 11 are Google balancing load across parallel routers.

Exit question

Local preference. Boise State gives routes learned from IRON a higher local preference, and every router in the AS prefers them. Local preference is the first rule in route selection, so it is decided before AS-PATH length is ever compared. BGP is policy first: who you pay, and how much, beats how short the path is.

Grading

Pass/fail, 20 points. Pass when every box has something in it. Wrong predictions are fine; that is what they are for.
