# G1 - Transmission Control Protocol

**Week 8 · 100 points · about 30 hours · submit in Canvas**

## Overview

You are going to write Transmission Control Protocol (TCP), including the handshake, the
teardown, retransmission, flow control and Reno congestion control. This is the biggest
milestone of the project and about 40% of the code you will write, so start early.

There is nothing under your TCP yet. It takes segments in and hands segments to a function
pointer, and your tests connect two copies of it through a simulator that can lose,
duplicate, reorder and corrupt them. The simulator's clock only moves when you tell it to,
so a test that covers a 60 second timeout takes no time, and the same seed gives the same
damage every time.

Keep working in your `cs525-g` repository and set `MILESTONE` to 1 in `tests/main-test.c`.

::: danger

Your code must compile on **GitHub Codespaces and Onyx**. If it compiles on only one of
them you will receive a zero even if it works on the other.

:::

## Background

![The same stack under your tests: your Unity tests drive two copies of your stack through the simulator](./g-harness.svg)

The interface in `src/stack/tcp.h` is fixed, and the comment on each function is its
specification. The `tcb_t` and `tcp_t` structs are yours, so add whatever you need to
them. `tcb_t` is short for transmission control block, the name
[RFC 9293](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc9293) gives the state it keeps for one
connection. Nothing allocates memory, since the kernel has no allocator. That is why
`TCP_MAX_CONNS` and `TCP_BUF_SIZE` are fixed sizes.

Read `tests/smoke-test.c` before you start. Its `tcp_pump()` is a whole network in five
lines: move the clock, hand TCP whatever arrived, and tell TCP what time it is. Every test
you write for this milestone starts from something like it.

```c
static void tcp_pump(void) {
  static uint8_t seg[SIM_MAX_PACKET];
  size_t n;
  sim_advance(&wire, 1);
  while ((n = sim_recv(&wire, 0, seg, sizeof(seg))) > 0) tcp_input(&tcp_a, ADDR_B, ADDR_A, seg, n);
  while ((n = sim_recv(&wire, 1, seg, sizeof(seg))) > 0) tcp_input(&tcp_b, ADDR_A, ADDR_B, seg, n);
  tcp_tick(&tcp_a, sim_now(&wire));
  tcp_tick(&tcp_b, sim_now(&wire));
}
```

The specifications you need are RFC 9293 for the protocol,
[RFC 6298](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc6298) for the retransmission timer,
[RFC 5681](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc5681) for congestion control and
[RFC 5961](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc5961) for blind reset attacks. RFC stands for
Request for Comments. They are easier to read than their reputation, and the state tables
in RFC 9293 section 3.10 are close to pseudocode.

## Task 1 - Segments

Build and parse the 20 byte header, the checksum and the options. The flags you will use are
SYN (synchronize), ACK (acknowledge), FIN (finish) and RST (reset).

Your segments carry the Maximum Segment Size (MSS) option on a SYN and nothing else. Your
parser has to walk every option a peer might send and skip the ones it does not know,
because Linux offers window scaling, timestamps and selective acknowledgment on every
connection. The peer's option lengths can be anything at all, so make sure a length of 0
or one that runs off the end of the header can not hang you or read past the segment.

A segment with a bad checksum or too short for its header is dropped with no reply. A
segment for no connection gets a RST, unless it is a RST. Do your sequence number
comparisons with signed subtraction, since sequence numbers wrap at 2^32 and a plain `<`
is wrong for half of them. A test that starts with an initial sequence number just under
the wrap, which `tcp_init()` lets you pick, will find every one of these.

## Task 2 - Open and close

Implement all 11 states, including a simultaneous open and a simultaneous close.

`tcp_listen()`, `tcp_accept()` and `tcp_connect()` never block. `tcp_close()` sends a FIN
after everything already queued and gives up the handle, and TCP finishes the close on its
own. `TIME_WAIT` lasts 2 times `TCP_MSL_MS`, and a peer that never sends its FIN gets given
up on after `TCP_FIN_WAIT_2_MS`.

A server that closes first leaves a `TIME_WAIT` behind for every request, and 8 slots run
out fast. When the table is full, let the oldest `TIME_WAIT` give way. You will thank
yourself for this in G4.

Validate resets the way RFC 5961 says to. A RST exactly at the next expected sequence
number resets the connection, one elsewhere in the window gets a challenge ACK, and one
outside the window is dropped. Otherwise anyone who can guess roughly where your window is
can kill your connection.

## Task 3 - Reliable delivery

Hold on to bytes until they are acknowledged, retransmit them when they time out, and put
arriving segments back in order.

- Compute the retransmission timeout (RTO) the way RFC 6298 says to, starting at
  `TCP_RTO_INIT_MS`. It keeps a smoothed round trip time and its variance, and it stays
  between `TCP_RTO_MIN_MS` and `TCP_RTO_MAX_MS`.
- Never measure the round trip time of a segment you sent twice, because you can not tell
  which copy the ACK is for. That is Karn's algorithm.
- Double the RTO on every timeout, and abort the connection after `TCP_MAX_RETRIES`.
- Keep segments that arrive out of order and acknowledge what you have in order, so the
  duplicate ACKs tell the sender what is missing.

Your receive buffer can overlap anything. A retransmission can start before what you have
and end after it, so test that. Corrupted segments are the checksum's job, and your tests
should prove that with the simulator's `corrupt` rate.

## Task 4 - Flow control

Never send past the window your peer advertised, and advertise only the room you have.

When the peer's window goes to zero there is nothing to acknowledge, so nothing will ever
tell you it opened back up. The ACK that opens it can be lost, which leaves both sides
waiting on each other forever. Send a one byte probe on a timer that backs off, the way
RFC 9293 section 3.8.6.1 says to. A peer that answers with a zero window is not ignoring
you, so keep probing for as long as it answers and do not count these against
`TCP_MAX_RETRIES`. Test this with a reader that stops reading for a minute of simulated
time.

## Task 5 - Reno congestion control

Implement slow start, congestion avoidance, fast retransmit and fast recovery from RFC 5681.

| Event | `ssthresh` | Congestion window (`cwnd`) |
| ----- | ---------- | --------------------------- |
| Connection starts | `TCP_BUF_SIZE` | `min(4 * mss, max(2 * mss, 4380))` |
| New ACK, below `ssthresh` | no change | grows by what was acknowledged, up to 1 MSS |
| New ACK, above `ssthresh` | no change | grows by about 1 MSS per round trip |
| Third duplicate ACK | `max(flight / 2, 2 * mss)` | `ssthresh + 3 * mss`, and retransmit |
| More duplicate ACKs | no change | grows by 1 MSS |
| ACK of new data in recovery | no change | back down to `ssthresh` |
| First timeout of a segment | `max(flight / 2, 2 * mss)` | 1 MSS, and retransmit |
| Another timeout of that segment | no change | 1 MSS, and retransmit |

`tcp_get_info()` is the window into all of this, so assert on it. For example, a third
duplicate ACK with 64240 bytes in flight and an MSS of 1460 has to leave `ssthresh` at
32120 and `cwnd` at 36500. Make sure and test that one number for each row of the table.

## Task 6 - Test it under damage

Move real data through the simulator and compare what comes out to what went in. Here is
what I saw with the reference solution, moving 500 KB in simulated time, so you know
roughly what to expect.

| Damage | Simulated time |
| ------ | -------------- |
| 1% loss | about 3 seconds |
| 5% loss | 4.4 to 6.7 seconds |
| 20% loss | 51 to 265 seconds |
| 5% loss, 5% duplicates, 10% reordered, 1% corrupted | about 11 seconds |

Your tests must include all four, each with bytes that come out identical, and try a few
seeds. A loop that gives up after a simulated time limit will keep a stuck connection from
hanging your test run. Your test binary should still finish in seconds, since nothing is
really waiting.

## Task 7 - Coverage and leaks

```bash
make clean
make all
make report
make leak-test
```

Get `src/stack/tcp.c` to 100%. The report still lists the stubs for later milestones, so
ignore the total. You may only exclude branches originating from system or library calls.

100% is harder here than on any project so far, and that is the point. The lines you can
not reach with a clean run are the recovery paths, and the only way to reach them is to
build a test that forces each one. Hand-built segments through `tcp_input()` are the easy
way to do it, because you control every field.

## Instructor Notes

Instructor note, not shown to students.

**The solution's `tests/tcp-test.c` will not compile against student code.** 32 of its
lines reach into `tcb_t` to force states that are hard to get to from outside, and students
own that struct. Only `tests/smoke-test.c` and tests on the public functions run as they
are, so grade rows 2 to 5 by reading the student's tests and running the fixed interface
tests. If that turns out to be too much reading, the fix is to rewrite those 32 lines with
hand-built segments.

**The golden test is the check that this talks to a real TCP.** `tests/golden-test.c`
replays segments captured from a Linux client, one clean run of 16 segments and one lossy
run of 64. It stays out of the starter, and 1 of its lines reaches into `tcb_t`.

**Where it will go wrong for students.** I expect sequence comparisons that fail across the
wrap, a persist timer that is never tested, and RTO measured on a retransmission. An
unmixed simulator seed also lost the first packet every time while I was building this, so
a test that always fails on one seed is worth a second look at the test itself.

**Measured size.** The solution is 714 lines of `tcp.c` and 54 of `tcp.h`, against 1715
lines of tests. 30 hours is an estimate and nobody has done this yet.
